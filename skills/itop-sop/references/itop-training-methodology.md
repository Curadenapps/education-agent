## Core Pedagogical Principle

**Oral hygiene must be taught as a skilled behaviour.**

This principle changes the instructor's task from transmitting knowledge to shaping performance. Once we accept that brushing, flossing, and interdental cleaning involve fine motor control, sensory discrimination, and repeated practice, then teaching must be organised accordingly. This is the foundation of T2T. T2T only makes sense within a pedagogical model that recognises that learners need not only to hear and see, but also to experience and reproduce.

**What instructors are really teaching — three things simultaneously:**

| Layer | What it means |
|-------|--------------|
| **Understanding** | The learner needs a clear reason to care |
| **Movement skill** | The learner must develop control of angle, pressure, rhythm, entry path, and adaptation to different sites |
| **Behavioural integration** | The learner must be able to repeat the technique at home, with real constraints, without supervision |

A successful session therefore moves from: **Meaning → Movement → Maintenance**

## The iTOP Model of Skill Acquisition

Instructors need a model of skill acquisition because teaching becomes inconsistent when based only on intuition. Learners do not master oral hygiene techniques in a single step — they move through recognisable phases, each with different needs, different vulnerabilities, and different instructional priorities.

This model is informed by the Fitts & Posner (1967) framework of motor learning and is applied throughout iTOP instructor training.

### Stage 1: Cognitive Phase
The learner is trying to understand what to do.

**Typical learner state:** Hesitant, variable movement, high attention demand, easily overwhelmed

**Instructor priorities:**
- Create relevance and motivation
- Define one simple goal
- Introduce the technique with safety guardrails
- Reduce confusion
- Correct only the most important errors

**Instructor behaviours:**
- Use simple language
- Demonstrate slowly
- Name only the key targets
- Give immediate safety-oriented correction when needed
- Avoid overloading the learner with detail

---

### Stage 2: Associative Phase
The learner begins to coordinate the movement more consistently.

**Typical learner state:** Fewer large errors, better rhythm, more self-correction, still fragile under change

**Instructor priorities:**
- Stabilise a minimally correct version of the skill
- Build repetition with low cognitive load
- Introduce small variations once one success is achieved
- Reduce verbal dependence

**Instructor behaviours:**
- Let the learner repeat several attempts
- Delay feedback slightly when safe
- Give short feedback focused on result first, technique second
- Introduce variability: another site, another angle, mirror use, slower rhythm

---

### Stage 3: Autonomous Phase
The movement becomes easier, more stable, and less effortful.

**Typical learner state:** Technique is stabilising, more self-correcting, approaching independent use

**Instructor priorities:**
- Support transfer to real-life daily use
- Maintain quality with periodic checks
- Prevent relapse
- Connect behaviour to identity and routine

**Instructor behaviours:**
- Use check-ins instead of constant correction
- Reinforce consistency
- Help build cues and habit chains
- Prepare for barriers: time pressure, discomfort, boredom, or loss of motivation

## Minimum Viable Correctness

One of the most common teaching errors in clinical education is to pursue technical perfection too early. The principle of minimum viable correctness protects against this.

**Definition:** The first objective is not elegance, speed, or full mastery — but the establishment of a movement that is safe, functional, and repeatable.

**For all iTOP techniques, minimum viable correctness means:**
- Tissue-safe contact
- Correct entry to the target area
- Stable and gentle pressure
- Repeatable rhythm
- Ability to reproduce the movement independently

> **Rule:** Before refining elegance, first secure a safe and functional version of the movement. Do not demand perfection too early. First achieve safe control. Then refine.

This principle is especially important in T2T because touch-guided learning should first help the learner experience a correct and non-traumatic version of the action. Only once this baseline is stable should the instructor refine subtler aspects of performance. In practice, minimum viable correctness improves confidence, reduces cognitive overload, and makes learning more achievable.

## Feedback Methodology

Feedback is one of the instructor's most powerful tools — and one of the easiest to misuse. In T2T and oral hygiene instruction, learners depend on feedback to distinguish between what feels merely familiar and what is actually correct, safe, and effective.

**The feedback problem:** Too much feedback creates dependency. The learner may perform well during instruction but fail later when alone. The guidance hypothesis — supported by substantial evidence from motor learning research (Zurich lecture, Mário Rui Araújo PhD) — demonstrates that high-frequency feedback has detrimental effects on long-term retention. Strategic, less frequent feedback is more effective for long-term behaviour change and maintenance.

**Guiding rule:** Use enough feedback to preserve safety and direction, but not so much that the learner stops self-calibrating.

### Practical Feedback Sequence
1. Set one clear target
2. Allow the learner to attempt
3. Observe silently when safe
4. Give result-focused feedback first
5. Add one brief technique cue only if needed
6. Let the learner try again
7. Fade feedback as control improves

### Feedback Priority Tiers

| Priority | Questions |
|----------|-----------|
| **First: Safety** | Is the movement traumatic? Is force too high? Is the entry wrong? |
| **Second: Task success** | Did the tool reach the intended site? Was the movement smooth enough to be repeatable? |
| **Third: Refinement** | Angle, rhythm, economy of motion, adaptation to anatomy |

### Feedback Language Style
**Preferred style:** Short, concrete, actionable, calm, non-shaming

**Examples of good feedback:**
- *"Gentler."*
- *"Same site, slower rhythm."*
- *"Good entry. Less pressure now."*
- *"That's it — just hold that angle."*

**Avoid:** Long explanations during practice, multiple corrections at once, shaming or comparing learners, excessive praise that does not distinguish improvement from error

## The Practical Session Structure

### Opening every practical block
- Brief framing: what technique will be practised, why it matters clinically, and what the main learning focus will be
- Explanation should be short and targeted — the majority of the session must remain dedicated to hands-on practice
- Demonstration precedes practice, but must be concise: highlight only the essential movement, positioning, pressure, angulation, or sequence

### During practice
- Close supervision from the start
- Corrections focus first on safety and basic effectiveness: fit, pressure, controlled movement, correct orientation
- Secondary refinements introduced later once minimum viable correctness is established
- Participants alternate between performer and observer/guide roles where possible — this builds pedagogical awareness alongside personal skill

### Session rhythm
Demonstration → Practice → Observation → Correction → Repetition

Avoid prolonged interruptions. If many participants are struggling with the same issue, pause briefly to address the group rather than repeating the same correction many times.

### Closing each block
- Brief consolidation: summarise the key message, the most common errors, and the link to the three iTOP criteria
- Without this, participants may remember isolated techniques but miss the seminar's deeper educational logic

## Time Management in iTOP Seminars

Time management should serve learning quality, not only schedule control. The goal is not simply to "finish the programme" — it is to protect the educational rhythm required for participants to understand, experience, practise, and reflect.

**Core principle:** Practical time should not be sacrificed easily. If delays occur, reduce non-essential repetition or secondary detail before compressing T2T time. A seminar that covers everything superficially is less valuable than one that teaches fewer elements with greater clarity and retention.

### Timing rules

| Block type | Maximum duration |
|-----------|-----------------|
| Uninterrupted theory (no active learning) | 45 minutes |
| Single T2T session | 90 minutes |
| Day 2 Advanced afternoon | Shorter, more active-reflective format |

**If a block includes active learning strategies** (polling, case-based interaction, short exercises, discussion), its duration may be extended accordingly. The rule applies to uninterrupted exposition only.

### Introductory seminar time management
Respect the pedagogical sequence: theory → practical session → lunch → practical session → closing theory. This rhythm allows conceptual framework first, then embodied practice, then theoretical integration. Do not compress or reorder.

### Advanced seminar time management
More flexible and responsive to programme and participant needs. The trainer may spend more time on discussion, correction, or difficult cases. The objective is not to rush through more material, but to deepen judgement, skill, and coaching capacity.

## The Trainer's Role

Trainers are not presenters of techniques or product information. Their role is to translate the scientific, educational, and clinical logic of iTOP into a learning experience that participants can understand, trust, practise, and later apply in their own professional settings.

**The four instructor functions:**
1. **Content guide** — able to explain the clinical rationale for biofilm control and the logic of the iTOP system
2. **Practical facilitator** — able to structure and manage T2T sessions, give calibrated feedback, and support motor skill development
3. **Relational model** — embodying the communication style consistent with behaviour-oriented coaching
4. **Pedagogical coach** — helping participants develop their own ability to teach and coach patients

### Facilitation principles

**Non-dogmatic tone:** iTOP should not be facilitated as if there is only one acceptable script or inflexible way of teaching every patient. The goal is meaningful understanding, not blind adherence.

**Establish relevance early:** Participants must understand that the seminar is not merely about new tools, but about rethinking how biofilm control is taught, learned, and sustained.

**Elicit–Provide–Elicit:** Ask permission or explore what the participant already thinks → provide concise information → ask for their response or interpretation. This keeps learning collaborative and reduces resistance.

**Avoid the righting reflex:** Do not immediately correct, persuade, or "fix" every hesitation. Listen carefully, ask questions, and help participants articulate what they notice, what feels difficult, and what seems to work better.

**When resistance appears:** Do not confront it aggressively. Use rolling with resistance — acknowledge the concern, reflect it, and find the element of ambivalence that opens discussion.

### COMPASS facilitation attitudes
Modelled on motivational interviewing principles, iTOP instructors should embody:
- **Partnership** — work with participants rather than in an expert-recipient model
- **Acceptance** — empathy and respect for autonomy
- **Compassion** — prioritise the learner's needs
- **Evocation** — draw out the participant's own reflections and motivations rather than imposing them

## Engagement Techniques

Engagement in iTOP is not entertainment. It is a pedagogical tool — used purposefully to deepen understanding, create shared starting points, and help participants experience the logic of iTOP rather than merely hear it.

**Appropriate engagement formats and contexts:**

| Format | Best use |
|--------|---------|
| Sticky notes / Post-it | Surfacing prior knowledge or concerns at session opening |
| Mentimeter / polling | Clarifying distribution of participants' views before introducing a concept |
| Balloons / Lego / physical props | Making abstract concepts (like behavioural fragility) concrete and tangible |
| Short contrasting demonstrations | Helping participants feel the difference between correct and incorrect technique |
| Pair work and peer observation | Building pedagogical awareness alongside personal skill |
| Case-based short questions | Shifting from technical prescription to clinical reasoning |

**Principle:** Engagement should align with the tone of iTOP — respectful, non-dogmatic, and reflective. Participants should feel invited into the learning process, not manipulated into participating.

**Long purely expository stretches must be broken** with interaction, reflection, or practical involvement. This is especially important because iTOP addresses behaviour, motor learning, and clinical adaptation — topics learned more effectively when participants are mentally and physically active rather than passive listeners.
