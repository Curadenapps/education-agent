# Educational Psychology Principles

Source: iTOP Training Methodology, Zurich Educators Skills Lecture (Mário Rui Araújo PhD), Advanced UK Lecture 3, Barbara Olejarová / Mario guidelines collaboration 2025, Schmidt et al. Motor Control and Learning (2018)
Applied to iTOP lesson plans, SOPs, and feedback synthesis. These principles inform how training is designed — they do not override clinical protocols.

---

## 1. Spaced Repetition

**Principle:** Learning is strengthened when practice is distributed over time rather than massed in a single session.

**Application in iTOP training:**
- Every lesson plan must schedule a revisit of the previous session's key skill
- Competency gates are checked at the start of the following session, not just at the end of the current one
- Recommended intervals: 1 week → 2 weeks → 1 month → 3 months for new technique retention

**Instructor prompt:** "Before we start today — let's quickly revisit what you practised last time."

---

## 2. Cognitive Load Theory

**Principle:** Working memory is limited. Overloading it with too much new information at once impairs learning.

**Application in iTOP training:**
- Theory blocks capped at **45 minutes maximum** before a break or format change
- For purely expository content (no interactive elements): cap at **15 minutes** before a hands-on anchor
- Introduce one micro-skill at a time (e.g., angulation before pressure before stroke)
- Use physical demonstration to offload cognitive processing — the body learns while the mind follows
- Avoid delivering protocol rationale before the learner has experienced the technique

**Instructor prompt:** "Don't think about the theory yet — just feel what happens when you change the angle."

---

## 3. Motor Learning — Three-Stage Skill Acquisition

**Principle:** Oral hygiene is a fine motor skill, not a knowledge domain. It develops through recognisable stages, each with different instructional needs. (Fitts & Posner, 1967; Schmidt et al., 2018)

**The patient learning curve is non-linear** — like learning a musical instrument or playing badminton, not like memorising information.

| Stage | Learner state | Key instruction priority |
|-------|--------------|--------------------------|
| **Cognitive** | Trying to understand what to do; hesitant, variable, easily overwhelmed | Create relevance; define one simple goal; safety guardrails only |
| **Associative** | Beginning to coordinate movement; fewer large errors; better rhythm | Stabilise minimum viable correctness; build repetition; reduce verbal interference |
| **Autonomous** | Stable movement, less effortful; beginning to self-correct | Support transfer to real life; build habit chains; prevent relapse |

**Problems specific to dental instruction vs. sport:**
- No immediate visible feedback
- No time for structured spaced practice
- No video analysis
- No visible improvement cycle
- Sessions are short and infrequent

This means dental instructors must be more deliberate about the little feedback time they have, and more strategic about what to correct.

## 4. The Guidance Hypothesis

**Principle:** High-frequency, constant feedback during practice has detrimental effects on long-term skill retention. Strategic, less frequent feedback is more effective for lasting behaviour change. (Wulf, 2007; Araújo, Zurich lecture)

**Application in iTOP training:**
- Do not correct every error in real time
- Allow silent observation periods when the learner is safe
- Shift from augmented feedback toward self-calibration as learning progresses
- Summary feedback after a practice block is often more effective than concurrent feedback during it
- The goal is a learner who can self-correct at home — not one who performs well only under supervision

**Instructor prompt:** "Try it on your own first — I'll watch and give you one thing at the end."

---

## 5. Deliberate Practice

**Principle:** Skill improvement comes from focused, effortful practice on specific weaknesses — not from repeating what is already comfortable.

**Application in iTOP training:**
- Each Practice block must name the specific micro-skill being practised
- Instructor identifies and targets the learner's weakest zone or movement — not the whole technique
- Feedback is specific and immediate: "Your zone 3 angulation dropped — let's do just zone 3 again"
- Comfort does not equal competency

**Instructor prompt:** "You've got zones 1–2 well. Let's spend the next 10 minutes on zone 4 specifically."

## 6. Motivational Interviewing (MI) in Clinical Education

**Principle:** Adults learn better when they connect the skill to their own values and patient outcomes — not when told to comply.

**Application in iTOP training:**
- Open the session with a patient story, not a protocol statement
- Ask "What difference do you think this would make for your patients?" before explaining the technique
- Reflect back the learner's own words
- Avoid directive language in feedback; use exploratory language
- Avoid the **righting reflex** — the urge to immediately correct, persuade, or "fix" every hesitation

**COMPASS facilitation attitudes** (MI-informed):
- **Partnership** — work with, not at
- **Acceptance** — empathy and respect for autonomy
- **Compassion** — prioritise the learner's needs
- **Evocation** — draw out the participant's own motivations rather than imposing them

**Elicit–Provide–Elicit pattern:** Ask what the participant already thinks → provide concise information → ask for their response or interpretation

**Instructor prompt:** "What are you noticing in your patients' BOB scores when you apply this consistently?"

## 7. Adult Learning Theory (Andragogy)

**Principle:** Adult learners are self-directed, experience-driven, and need to understand the relevance of what they are learning.

**Application in iTOP training:**
- Connect every technique to a clinical outcome the practitioner cares about
- Respect existing clinical experience — frame iTOP as a refinement, not a replacement
- Give learners agency in the feedback phase: "What do you think you'd change?"
- Avoid patronising language; frame assessments as professional development, not testing
- Non-dogmatic facilitation: the goal is meaningful understanding, not blind compliance

## 8. COM-B Model — Patient Behaviour Change

> Source: Barbara Olejarová / Mario guidelines collaboration, April 2025
> **Critical insight:** Traditional iTOP + BOB methodology assumes *explain → show data → patient acts*. Behavioural science evidence shows knowledge alone does not sustain behaviour. iTOP must shift from information delivery to behaviour architecture.

**Principle:** Behaviour only occurs when Capability, Opportunity, and Motivation are all present simultaneously.

| Component | Question to ask | iTOP application |
|-----------|----------------|-----------------| 
| **Capability** | Can the patient physically perform the technique? Do they have the right tool? | T2T is strong here — but capability must extend beyond the chair into the home routine |
| **Opportunity** | Does their daily environment support the behaviour? Is the brush visible, accessible, timed? | Currently absent from iTOP methodology — must be explicitly addressed |
| **Motivation** | Is the goal meaningful to *this patient*, in *their* frame? | Requires individual elicitation — not a standard motivational pitch |

Full patient application: see `patient-behaviour-change.md`

## 9. Micro Goals and Implementation Intentions

**Principle:** Specific, small, time-anchored action plans produce significantly higher follow-through than general instructions.

**Application — the one-thing rule:**
Every patient session must close with one specific implementation intention, not a full protocol instruction:
- Specific tool: *"the pink brush"*
- Specific location: *"the two gaps at the lower front"*
- Specific time anchor: *"after you brush tonight"*
- Expected sensation: *"it may feel tight — that's correct, don't force it"*

Example: *"After you brush tonight, use the pink brush in the two lower front gaps. It will take about 30 seconds."*

**Instructor prompt (for training clinicians):** "Don't send them home with the full chart. Send them home with one thing they can actually do tonight."

## 10. Follow-Up as the Behaviour Change Mechanism

**Principle:** Follow-up is not administrative — it is where most patient behaviour change actually occurs or fails. It must be structured as a coaching conversation, not a data review.

**Follow-up conversation structure:**
1. **Check the plan** — "Did you try the pink brush in those two gaps?" (not "how is your oral hygiene?")
2. **Celebrate or troubleshoot** — if yes: acknowledge specifically. If no: explore the obstacle without judgement
3. **Adjust** — make the plan easier if needed, or extend to one additional space if ready
4. **Re-anchor** — close with the next specific micro goal

**BOB scores in follow-up — reframed:**
- Lead with the patient's experience, not the number
- Use the number to confirm and celebrate: *"Your score went from 23 to 11 — that's what two minutes every evening looks like over three weeks."*
- Never open a follow-up by showing a chart before asking how it felt

**Instructor prompt:** "The score is yours to track. The story belongs to the patient — let them tell it first."

## 11. Behaviour Change — Chronic Conditions

**Principle:** Chronic oral health conditions are not resolved through isolated interventions but through sustained behaviours over time. The instructor's role shifts from instruction-giver to behavioural system designer.

**Application in Advanced iTOP:**
- Behaviour often breaks down between appointments — not due to lack of knowledge but due to: time pressure, discomfort, poor follow-up quality, weak therapeutic alliance, competing life demands
- Appointment A and Appointment B serve different functions (see `patient-behaviour-change.md`)
- Variability, relapse, and partial adherence are inherent features of chronic care — they require adaptive and supportive strategies, not corrective instructions alone

**Active learning application:** The Jenga Clinical Tower exercise (Advanced Day 1) makes this fragility visible and tangible before the lecture explains it conceptually.

## Reference Table for Session Design

| Design decision | Principle | Rule |
|----------------|-----------|------|
| Theory block length (expository) | Cognitive load | Max 45 min; max 15 min without interactive break |
| T2T session length | Motor learning | Max 90 min per session |
| Feedback frequency | Guidance hypothesis | Less is more once safety is assured; fade as control improves |
| First correction focus | Minimum viable correctness | Safety first, then task success, then refinement |
| Session close | Implementation intentions | One specific, time-anchored plan per patient |
| Follow-up opening | Behaviour change mechanism | Ask about the plan before showing the data |
| Advanced Day 2 afternoon | Cognitive load + reflection | Shorter, more active-reflective format |
